# The “Kill Line” in America and the Philosophy of Personal Freedom

Recently, a Chinese blogger who returned from the United States sparked widespread discussion about what has come to be known as the “kill line” in American society. Many people see this as a moment of disillusionment with the American dream. But I see it somewhat differently. Rather than revealing the superiority or failure of one particular system, it illustrates the differences between two ways of life and two distinct attitudes toward consumption and financial security.

Anyone who has traded stocks understands the concept of a margin call and forced liquidation. With a margin deposit of just 50,000 yuan, an investor can control a position worth several times that amount, or even more through leverage. But leverage cuts both ways. Once losses exceed the available margin, and the investor cannot provide additional funds, the position is forcibly liquidated.

In this sense, I believe the so-called “kill line” in American society is not fundamentally different from being liquidated in a leveraged financial market.

Compared with Chinese people, Americans are generally more accustomed to borrowing money to enjoy a better life today, relying on future income to finance present consumption. Chinese people, by contrast, have traditionally placed greater emphasis on saving, preferring to accumulate wealth before spending it. For many ordinary Chinese families, a mortgage is perhaps one of the few widely accepted forms of borrowing for consumption. Even this attitude toward debt has largely developed over the past two decades.

When a society built around credit and borrowing encounters unemployment, illness, or broader economic disruption, a lifestyle sustained by expectations of future income can collapse remarkably quickly. It is much like leveraged trading: when circumstances change and expectations fail to materialise, forced liquidation becomes inevitable.

However, I do not believe that saving is necessarily safer than borrowing. It depends on how we define security. If security simply means refusing to pursue returns and watching inflation and the passage of time gradually erode the purchasing power of your savings, that is certainly a choice. But I would not consider it genuine financial security.

Every social system, economic model, and way of life has its own advantages and disadvantages. We should not dismiss an entire country simply because we observe its shortcomings, nor should we overlook the risks of the society in which we happen to live. The sensible approach is to understand how different societies operate, manage our own risks, and make use of the advantages offered by different institutions and markets.

I favour a more global approach to personal resource allocation. There is no reason to tie our employment, income, investments, consumption, and everyday lives entirely to a single country. We can earn money in one place, invest in global markets, and choose to live somewhere else that offers a better quality of life. This is what I understand as geographical arbitrage.

Ultimately, I believe we should be loyal to our own lives, and above all, to ourselves — not blindly loyal to a particular country, political system, or way of life.

Understand the world, but do not idolise any particular system. Make use of the rules, but do not become a prisoner of them. Manage risks, but never abandon the pursuit of opportunity.

We do not need to prove which country is better, nor should we entrust our entire future to a single choice. What truly matters is to think independently, understand the differences in the world, recognise the opportunities they offer, and make ourselves people with the freedom to choose.
